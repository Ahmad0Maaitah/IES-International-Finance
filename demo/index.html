The questionWho decided a pound costs $1.32? Is that the right price?

Drag the slider: the same $1,000 at other rates this year.

0:20 to 1:20. Ask the room: "Who has changed money for a trip?" Point at the big number. "Every one of our students lives this. The same $1,000 bought £736 in February and £760 in November. Nobody voted on that price; the market set it, nine and a half trillion dollars of trades a day, most of it in this city."

Move the slider once so the number changes. Then land the question: "Is $1.32 the right price for a pound? Finance has a tool for that and it is a burger."

Do not explain the chart; it is scenery. The number is the point.

One burger, one price?

The law of one price: the same thing costs the same everywhere, once you convert. A Big Mac is the same thing everywhere.

New York

$6.12

The Economist, Big Mac index, January 2026.

London: your guess?

At $1.32 per pound the law of one price says £4.62. Does it?

Hands up, then Reveal.

1:20 to 2:50. "Same burger, same recipe, same company. If the exchange rate is 'right', the London price should be the New York price divided by the rate: $6.12 divided by 1.32 is £4.62. Hands up: who says £3.99? £4.62? £5.29? £6.49?" Click the tallies as hands go up, then Reveal.

The answer is £5.29 (The Economist, January 2026). Say: "So London charges 14 per cent more for the same burger. Either the burger is wrong or the exchange rate is. Economists bet on the exchange rate, and that is purchasing power parity: the rate that would make the burger cost the same in both places."

If someone picks £4.62, celebrate it: "That is exactly what the theory predicts. Reality disagrees, and the gap is today's lesson."

The Big Mac calculator

 
Prices: The Economist, January 2026 (editable). Rate: OFX, 15 October 2026. PPP rate = US price ÷ London price.

2:50 to 5:00. The centrepiece; give it the time. Walk through the three numbers once: "US price $6.12. London price £5.29. Divide: $1.16 per pound. That is the burger's opinion of what a pound should cost. The market says $1.32. The pound is 14 per cent overvalued: your dollars buy less burger here than at home."

Hand the slider to someone in the room, or do it yourself: drag to 1.16 ("fair value, the verdict goes green"), drag to 1.00 ("now London is the bargain, and American tourists flood in"), drag to 1.50 ("a dollar buys almost nothing here"). Press the Today button to return.

Point out for the room: "This is what interactive means in my classroom. The student changes a number and the picture answers. Nobody is copying a formula from a slide; they are using one."

If a new Economist edition is out, type the new prices into the two boxes live; the whole slide recomputes.

5:00 to 6:10. "Same exercise, fifty countries, one chart. Green bars are cheap for a dollar-holder; red bars are expensive. Quiz: where does a dollar buy the most burger?" Let the room choose; the correct option lights up green, a wrong one red, and the explanation appears.

Then the pattern: "Look at who is red: Switzerland, Norway, Britain, the euro area. Rich places. Look at who is green: India, Japan, China, Brazil. The index is not random, which is the clue to why the pound does not simply fall to $1.16."

So why doesn't the pound just fall to $1.16?

Money moves faster than menus

$9.6 trillion a day of currency trading (BIS, 2025). Burger prices change twice a year.

A compass, not a clock

Parity says where fair is, not when you get there.

Vote: by April, will a pound cost less than $1.32?

The ideaExchange rates wander; prices anchor. Purchasing power parity is the rate at which the same goods cost the same in two countries.

6:10 to 7:00. Two reasons, one line each: money trades $9.6 trillion a day, 38 per cent of it in the UK, and moves in seconds while menus change twice a year; parity is a compass, not a clock, so it says where fair is, not when you arrive. If asked for more: no burger arbitrage across borders, and rent, wages and VAT sit inside the price (the Balassa-Samuelson effect).

The vote: click the tallies as hands go up. Landing line: "You have just made a currency forecast. Parity gave you a target, $1.16; your instinct gave you a timing. Separating the two is the rest of week 4, and the group project." Then read the idea panel slowly and close: "Three things you saw: a guess, a slider and a vote. Every week of the course runs this way. Students leave with a number they computed themselves, not a definition they copied." Homework, if asked: price something you buy in London and at home, compute your own parity rate, post it on Moodle. Stop at 7:00 and open the syllabus from the toolbar.
