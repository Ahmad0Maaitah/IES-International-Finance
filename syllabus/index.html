Course description

Every student on this course arrived in London through a foreign exchange transaction. The dollars that paid for the flat, the Oyster card and the first coffee were sold for pounds at a price set in a market that turns over $9.6 trillion a day, more than a third of it in this city (BIS, 2025). International finance is the study of that market and of everything that depends on it: how countries settle their accounts with one another, how exchange rates are determined and forecast, how firms and investors manage the risk of a currency moving against them, and how multinational companies raise capital, invest and pay tax across borders.

The course follows the IES Abroad specification for FI 355 and covers the international monetary system and the balance of payments; the foreign exchange market and the parity conditions that tie exchange rates to prices and interest rates; currency derivatives and the management of transaction, economic and translation exposure; international banking, bond and equity markets and international portfolio investment; and the financial management of the multinational firm, from foreign direct investment and the cost of capital to cash management, trade finance and taxation. It closes with the questions that are moving the subject now: digital money, sanctions and the dollar, and climate risk.

Three commitments shape how it is taught. First, every concept lands on something students are living: the pound in their pocket, the City on their doorstep, the headline that morning. Second, every session is interactive in a specific sense: the student changes something (an exchange rate, a hedge, a correlation) and sees the consequence, in simulations that run in any browser without a login. Third, every number on a slide comes from a named source that students can open themselves. The result is a course that connects theory to real-world events because it is built from them, and that leaves students able to compute, hedge and argue a currency view rather than recite one.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the course, students will be able to:

  1. Explain how the international monetary system and the balance of payments shape exchange rates, and interpret current economic and political events in those terms.
  2. Read and compute foreign exchange quotations (spot, forward, cross rates, bid and ask) and apply the parity conditions (purchasing power parity, interest rate parity, the Fisher effects) to real market data.
  3. Measure a firm's transaction, economic and translation exposure and design a hedge using forwards, money market instruments or options, stating its cost and its limits.
  4. Describe the structure of international banking, bond and equity markets and evaluate the gains and risks of international portfolio diversification, including the currency component of returns.
  5. Appraise a cross-border investment: foreign direct investment, the cost of capital and capital budgeting in two currencies, with country risk, cash management, trade finance and tax taken into account.
  6. Test a theory of international finance against a real event using data, and communicate the analysis in a written report and an oral briefing to a non-specialist audience.

Method of presentation

The course runs in a seminar format built around three moves in every session. A concept is developed in about twenty minutes, worked on the board with one real number. An interaction follows: a browser simulation, a guess or poll, or a short case worked in pairs, in which the student changes something and the picture answers. A discussion closes the session: the week's "FX in the news" briefing by a student and the question of the week.

Each week has the same rhythm. Before class (60 to 90 minutes): the textbook pages for the week, one current article posted on Moodle from the Financial Times or The Economist, and a five-question warm-up quiz on the course site with instant feedback (not graded; it tells the instructor where to spend time). In class: the three moves above. After class: a short problem set with worked answers, the group project step for that week, and office hours.

Other elements: a course-related trip to the Bank of England Museum (week 3); a self-guided walk through the City with questions to answer; a student "FX in the news" rota so that every student briefs the room once during the term; a group research paper with two dated status updates; a mock midterm and a practice final with answers. Where a guest speaker from a London institution can be arranged, the session is scheduled in week 11 or 12 and the news briefing that week becomes the questions to the guest.

Why the interaction is not decorationAcross 225 studies, students in lecture-only courses were about 1.5 times more likely to fail than students in courses with active learning, and exam scores were 6 per cent higher with active learning (Freeman et al., 2014). Students in active classes also report that they feel they learn less even as they learn more (Deslauriers et al., 2019), so the reasoning behind the format is explained to the class in week 1.

Research paper guidelines

Choose the theory first and the event second. A menu of twelve topics opens in week 3, each with a one-page template, two example events and the chapter it rests on; groups may propose their own topic with the instructor's approval. Narrow the topic to one aspect of the theory rather than a survey. Find data (central bank statistics, The Economist's Big Mac index, company filings, IMF and BIS publications are all free) and analyse how the evidence fits the theory: does it, why or why not, and what follows. The analysis carries the most weight. Example topics: did purchasing power parity hold for the pound after the September 2022 mini-budget; the August 2024 yen carry-trade unwind and what interest rate parity predicted; Argentina's peso and the Big Mac; Apple's currency hedging programme as disclosed in its annual report; why the Bank of England could not defend the pound on Black Wednesday in 1992.

Determination of the overall course grade

Marks are weighted as above and converted to a letter grade according to the IES Abroad Academic Policy Guidelines. The Moodle gradebook is updated within a week of each assessment so that students always know where they stand.

Generative AI, academic integrity and accommodations

Generative AI tools may be used for brainstorming, language editing and help with spreadsheet or code syntax, and their use must be disclosed in one line at the end of the paper. The choice of theory, the data work and the analysis must be the group's own; the midterm and final are closed book, so the course grade measures the student. Plagiarism and other breaches are handled under the IES Abroad academic integrity policy. Students who require accommodations should contact IES Abroad London in the first week so that arrangements are in place before the first assessment.

Attendance policy

The IES Abroad London attendance policy applies. Attendance is mandatory for all classes, including the course-related trip. Each unexcused absence reduces the final grade by one half of a letter grade. Absences are excused only for serious illness (reported to the Centre before class) or a serious illness or death in the immediate family requiring travel (with the Centre Director's written approval). Arriving more than ten minutes late may be counted as an unexcused absence. Absences totalling 25 per cent of class hours lead to contact from the Academic Programmes Manager and the Centre Director and may lead to disciplinary review. Students should be aware that UK immigration rules require the Centre to monitor attendance and that poor attendance can affect a student's permission to remain on the programme. Full details are in the IES Abroad Academic Policy Guidelines and the Centre handbook.

Course-related trips

Bank of England Museum, Threadneedle Street (week 3). A visit to the museum of the UK's central bank: the gold vault exhibit, the history of the pound, the inflation target, and the 1992 and 2022 sterling episodes that the course uses as cases in weeks 2 and 6. Admission is free. The visit is part of the course and attendance is required; a short written reflection (not graded) feeds the week 4 discussion.

The City walk (self-guided, weeks 3 to 5). A route from Bank station past the Royal Exchange, the Bank of England and the Lloyd's building with questions to answer about who trades what, where. Students complete it in their own time and bring their answers to the week 5 session.

Optional. Public lectures at the London School of Economics and other London institutions on currencies, central banking and trade are announced on Moodle as they arise; attendance is encouraged and can be used for the news briefing.
