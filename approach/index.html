Who they are: most have one finance or economics course behind them, some have none, so weeks 1 and 2 build the shared language before anything is assumed.

Clarity: a syllabus with every date, reading and rubric on day one; the Moodle gradebook tells them where they stand within a week of each assessment. The City walk and the news rota

A self-guided walk with questions. One five-minute news briefing per student.

BIS Triennial Central Bank Survey, April 2025; Bank of England, UK results 2025.

The point for a non-finance room: the students are sitting in the capital of the subject. Nine and a half trillion dollars a day, more than a third of it within two Tube stops of the Centre.

The visit: Threadneedle Street, the gold bar, the 1992 and 2022 sterling stories, the inflation target; free and bookable. The walk: Bank, Royal Exchange, Lloyd's, with questions to bring to week 5. The rota: every student briefs the room once, from the Financial Times or The Economist that week.

Why the same rhythm every weekA student new to a city never has to guess what the course wants.

Before: the textbook pages for the week, one article from the Financial Times or The Economist on Moodle, and a warm-up quiz on the course site with instant feedback; it tells me what to spend time on. In class: the concept is worked on the board with one real number; the interaction is a simulation, a poll or a case in pairs; the discussion is the student news briefing and the question of the week. After: worked answers on Moodle and the group project step (topic, data, draft). Everything is posted before the session.

The one-liner: predictability is a kindness to a student who is 3,000 miles from home.

Why interactive: the evidence

Share of students failing, 225 studies. Freeman et al. (2014), PNAS.
The meta-analysisLecture-only students were 1.5 times more likely to fail. Exam scores 6 per cent higher with active learning.
The catchActive learners learn more but feel they learn less (Deslauriers et al., 2019). I tell them in week 1.
The rule I build toThe student changes something and sees the consequence. Watching me click is not interactive.

Freeman et al. (2014), PNAS: 225 studies of undergraduate science, engineering and mathematics courses; failure rates 34 per cent under lecturing against 22 per cent with active learning; exam scores 6 per cent higher. Deslauriers et al. (2019), PNAS: students in active classes learn more but rate their learning lower, so tell them in advance that effort will feel like confusion and they rate the course for what they learned, not how smooth it felt.

And the subject: finance is a doing discipline; you learn a hedge by placing one and a forecast by making one, safely, in class. In my classroom that means simulations the students drive, live data with the source on the slide, ungraded guesses and votes, and cases from the news. Every session has a moment where the room, not the lecturer, produces the number.

Weights are the IES course specification; what changes is how each component is run. Participation is one five-minute "FX in the news" briefing per student plus engagement in the activities, scored on a rubric. The paper has two dated status updates with written feedback within a week. The AI line is deliberate: American students expect a stated policy, and a stated policy is enforceable; the analysis must be the group's own.

The paper is de-risked by design: theory first from a menu of twelve topics in week 3, then two dated updates (week 5 the question and the theory, week 8 the data and a first chart) with written feedback. A group with a question in week 5 and a chart in week 8 cannot fail in week 13. Example topics: parity and the pound after the 2022 mini-budget; the 2024 yen carry-trade unwind; Argentina's peso and the Big Mac; Apple's hedging read from its 10-K.
