Course facts from the IES Abroad FI 355 course page (3 credits, 45 contact hours, no prerequisites, English); the term runs 18 January to 23 April 2027.

Three promises to the students

Upper-level US undergraduates, one semester in London, most with one finance or economics course behind them and some with none. The course has no prerequisite, so weeks 1 and 2 build the shared language before anything is assumed.

Clarity

A syllabus with every date, reading and rubric on day one, and the same three-move rhythm in every session. Students always know what to do next, and the Moodle gradebook tells them where they stand within a week of each assessment.

Connection

Every concept lands on something they are living: the pound in their pocket, the City on their doorstep, the headline that morning. The IES brief is to connect theory to real-world events; London makes that easy if the course is built for it.

Confidence

They leave able to read a currency quote, price a hedge, test a theory against data and argue a currency view, with a 3,500-word group paper they can show an employer and a transcript grade they understand.

What it needsNothing here needs special equipment: a projector, a browser and Moodle. The course site works on a phone and offline once opened.

Keep to 60 seconds. The three words are the spine of everything that follows: clarity (structure), connection (London and the news), confidence (what they can do by April).

Course-related trip: Bank of England Museum

Week 3, Threadneedle Street: the gold bar, the 1992 and 2022 sterling stories, the inflation target. Free entry, a short walk from Bank station, and the anchor for the "defend the peg" case the following week.

The City walk and the daily news

A self-guided walk (Bank, Royal Exchange, Lloyd's) with questions to answer, and a rota: every student gives one five-minute "FX in the news" briefing during the term, from the Financial Times or The Economist that week.

Sources: BIS Triennial Central Bank Survey, press release 30 September 2025; Bank of England, summary of UK results, 2025 ($4,745 billion a day, 37.8 per cent of global turnover).

The point for a non-finance room: the students are sitting in the capital of the subject. Nine and a half trillion dollars a day, more than a third of it within two Tube stops of the Centre. The visit is free and bookable; the walk needs nothing.

How a week runs

Before class · 60 to 90 minutes

Prepare

The textbook pages for the week, one current article posted on Moodle (Financial Times or The Economist) and a five-question warm-up quiz on the course site with instant feedback. Not graded; it tells me what to spend time on.

In class · three moves every session

Do

Concept in twenty minutes, worked on the board with one real number. Interaction: a simulation, a poll or a case in pairs, where the student changes something and the picture answers. Discussion: the news briefing and the question of the week.

After class · by the next session

Practise

A short problem set with worked answers on Moodle, the group project step for that week (topic, data, draft) and office hours twice a week. Everything posted before the session, nothing hidden until after.

What this gives a student abroadPredictability. The same rhythm every week means a student who is also finding a flat, a Tube route and a new city never has to guess what the course wants.

Say the three moves out loud: concept, interaction, discussion. Then the one-liner: predictability is a kindness to a student who is 3,000 miles from home.

Why interactive: the evidence

225 studies of undergraduate science, engineering and mathematics courses; exam scores 6 per cent higher with active learning. Freeman et al. (2014), PNAS.
The meta-analysisStudents in lecture-only courses were 1.5 times more likely to fail than students in courses with active learning (Freeman et al., 2014).
The catchStudents in active classes learn more but feel they learn less (Deslauriers et al., 2019). So in week 1 I show them this slide and say why the room works this way.
And the subjectFinance is a doing discipline: you learn a hedge by placing one and a forecast by making one, safely, in class, before any money is at stake.

Two citations, both in PNAS, both large. The Deslauriers finding is the one that matters for course evaluations: tell students in advance that effort will feel like confusion, and they rate the course for what they learned, not how smooth it felt.

What interactive looks like in my classroom

Simulations

The student changes a number and the picture answers: the Big Mac calculator, a "hedge or not" game with a real invoice, covered interest arbitrage with today's rates. In the browser, no login, phone-friendly.

Live data

Today's dollar-pound rate, this week's Bank of England or Federal Reserve decision, the latest Big Mac prices. Every number on a slide comes from a named source, and the source is on the slide.

Polls, guesses and quizzes

Guess the London price before I reveal it, vote on where the pound goes by April, check yourself with instant explanations. Wrong answers are the best teaching moments and nobody is graded on them.

Cases from the news

Black Wednesday 1992, the pound after the September 2022 mini-budget, the August 2024 yen carry-trade unwind. Each case is a theory tested against something that actually happened.

The rule I build toInteractive means the student changes something and sees the consequence. Watching me click is not interactive. Every session has a moment where the room, not the lecturer, produces the number.

If time is short, read only the rule in the callout. The four cards are examples the room can scan.

The research paper, de-risked

What usually goes wrong with a 40 per cent paperGroups pick an event first and hunt for a theory afterwards, start in week 10, and hand in a description rather than an analysis.
What I do insteadTheory first: a menu of twelve topics in week 3, each with a one-page template and two example events. Status update one (week 5) is the question and the theory; update two (week 8) is the data and a first chart. Written feedback within a week of each.

Example topics on the menu

Did purchasing power parity hold for the pound after the September 2022 mini-budget?

The August 2024 yen carry-trade unwind: what interest rate parity predicted and what happened.

Argentina's peso and the Big Mac: can a burger spot a devaluation?

Apple's currency hedging programme, read from its annual 10-K filing.

Black Wednesday 1992: why the Bank of England could not defend the pound.

Every topic maps to one chapter of Eun, Resnick and Chuluun (2024) and to data students can download for free (central banks, The Economist, company filings).

The de-risking is the two dated updates. A group that has a question in week 5 and a chart in week 8 cannot fail the paper in week 13.

Supporting students a long way from home

Structure they recognise

A syllabus with every date, rubrics for every graded item, letter grades through the IES Abroad scale, Moodle updated before each session, a gradebook never a week behind.

Access

Office hours twice a week at the Centre, email answered within one working day, accommodations arranged through IES London from the first week, quietly.

Belonging

Groups formed in week 1, the Bank of England visit in week 3 as a shared anchor, and a briefing rota so every student speaks to the room at least once about something they chose.

Honesty

The IES attendance, integrity and AI policies are explained in week 1 with examples, not just printed. Expectations stated early are expectations met.

Experience I bringModule lead for undergraduate finance modules at the University of Southampton and for an executive FinTech programme in Muscat; mixed cohorts; a habit of writing the course down so clearly that a student who missed a week can catch up alone.

Experience with American students and familiarity with US higher-education culture: show them here with the concrete mechanics (syllabus, rubrics, gradebook, office hours) rather than a claim.

The first three weeks, in practice

Before day one

Moodle built: syllabus with dates, readings, rubrics, warm-up quizzes. The course site live. The Bank of England visit booked. A welcome note with the one-page "how this course works".

Week 1

Why international finance, why London. Groups formed, the news-briefing rota agreed, and every student opens a currency diary: what did your $1,000 buy this week?

Week 2

The monetary system and the balance of payments. The Black Wednesday case and the "defend the peg" simulation. First warm-up results tell me who needs the maths clinic.

Week 3

The FX market: reading quotes, spot and forward, a triangular arbitrage hunt with live prices. Bank of England Museum visit. The paper topic menu opens.

By week 4Every student has computed their own purchasing power parity rate for something they buy, and the room has argued about whether the pound is too expensive. That session is the ten-minute demonstration that follows.
